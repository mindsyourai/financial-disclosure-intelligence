===== SLIDE 1 =====
From Model Disagreement | to Disclosure Intelligence | An Agentic Decision Support System for Financial Narratives | Kazim Topuz (Presenter) | Jessica Bedson | Tally Ferguson  | Thomas Kim | The University of Tulsa  |  DSI 2026 | Core idea: preserve disagreement, diagnose it, and turn it into decision-relevant evidence. | 1
-- NOTES: Open with the problem, not the technology. Annual reports contain valuable information, but they are long, repetitive, legally conditioned, and difficult to interpret consistently. Our central idea is simple: when credible analytical methods disagree, that disagreement should not automatically be averaged away. ADAM preserves it, diagnoses it, and uses it as evidence. This presentation focuses on the DSS architecture, the evaluation design, and preliminary validation results. | 1

===== SLIDE 2 =====
MOTIVATION | The decision problem: finding what matters inside a 10-K | Volume | Narrative sections are long and costly to review paragraph by paragraph. | Repetition | Material language is mixed with prior-year carryover, boilerplate, and legal caution. | Interpretive difficulty | Tone, economic direction, and informativeness are not the same construct. | The practical DSS question | Which passages contain new, firm-specific, decision-relevant information — and which passages require closer human judgment? | Motivated by disclosure informativeness, readability, boilerplate, and financial-text analytics literatures. | 2
-- NOTES: The problem is not merely sentiment classification. A decision maker has to find a small number of consequential passages inside lengthy filings. Some negative language is routine risk-factor boilerplate; some neutral language describes a genuinely adverse event. The DSS challenge is to distinguish tone, economic implication, and incremental informativeness while retaining enough evidence for a human to inspect the result. | 2

===== SLIDE 3 =====
RESEARCH GAP | Why existing financial-text approaches are not enough | 1. Partial views | LM is transparent but context-blind. FinBERT reads semantic context but is less directly traceable. Readability measures answer a different question. | 2. Missing disclosure context | Standalone sentiment methods do not tell us whether language is new, persistent, peer-common, firm-specific, or boilerplate. | 3. False precision | A single class or score can hide genuine conflict. Users get little guidance about where interpretation is difficult or why. | Gap in DSS design | How should heterogeneous analytical evidence be coordinated when credible methods produce complementary or conflicting signals? | Loughran & McDonald (2011); Araci (2019); Bochkay et al. (2023); Brown & Tucker (2011). | 3
-- NOTES: We frame three gaps. First, the major analytical approaches see different aspects of the same disclosure. Second, sentiment methods generally lack temporal and peer context that is essential for evaluating informativeness. Third, conventional outputs can create false precision: a difficult paragraph and an easy paragraph may both receive one confident-looking class. These gaps motivate an evidence-coordination problem rather than a search for one universally best classifier. | 3

===== SLIDE 4 =====
CORE IDEA | Model disagreement can be information — not just error | LM says | NEGATIVE | FinBERT says | NEUTRAL | WHY? | Negation | “not expected to…” | Legal language | litigious terms ≠ distress | Boilerplate | repeated risk language | Conditionality | could / may / if | Mixed statements | bad history + good outlook | Factual events | neutral wording, adverse economics | Agreement does not guarantee correctness; disagreement does not necessarily imply ambiguity. | Disagreement mechanisms synthesized from the paper’s theoretical grounding. | 4
-- NOTES: This is the conceptual pivot of the paper. A negative dictionary score and a neutral transformer score may both be understandable because they respond to different properties of the text. Negation, legal terminology, boilerplate, conditionality, mixed statements, and factual reporting are recurring mechanisms. The system should diagnose these cases rather than average them. Importantly, disagreement and uncertainty are not the same thing: sometimes the disagreement is resolvable once we add the right context. | 4

===== SLIDE 5 =====
RESEARCH DESIGN | Four research questions — four design requirements | RQ1 | How can a DSS coordinate lexicon, transformer, and contextual-quality evidence? | RQ2 | Does agentic adjudication improve interpretation, and is the gain architectural? | RQ3 | What explains disagreement, and does it identify passages needing review? | RQ4 | Are confidence-aware outputs useful for prioritization and economically relevant? | Design requirements | DR1 | Preserve heterogeneous evidence | DR2 | Diagnose disagreement | DR3 | Qualify signals with temporal/disclosure context | DR4 | Maintain traceability to source text | 5
-- NOTES: The research questions and design requirements are deliberately aligned. RQ1 is the design question. RQ2 asks whether the architecture adds value beyond its components. RQ3 focuses on disagreement as a diagnostic phenomenon. RQ4 moves from predictive accuracy to decision-support behavior and economic relevance. The four design requirements then become the criteria that the architecture and evaluation need to satisfy. | 5

===== SLIDE 6 =====
ARTIFACT | ADAM: six layers from disclosure to decision support | 1 | Disclosure & Decision Context | 10-K Item 1A / Item 7 • firm / year / section / sector | 2 | Structuring & Contextualization | section extraction • paragraph objects • prior-year / peer links | 3 | Specialized Evidence Agents | LM lexicon • FinBERT • Context & Quality | 4 | Intelligent Adjudication | assess convergence • diagnose conflict • retain ambiguity | 5 | Disclosure Intelligence Synthesis | paragraph → section → company-year measures | 6 | Decision-Support Outputs | profiles • alerts • drill-down • explanations | Agentic ≠ every component is an LLM. Agentic = specialized roles + structured evidence + explicit coordination. | 6
-- NOTES: ADAM is a six-layer DSS architecture. The first two layers establish the information environment and create traceable paragraph objects. Layer 3 produces three distinct forms of evidence. Layer 4 adjudicates rather than votes. Layer 5 aggregates while maintaining links to the underlying paragraphs. Layer 6 exposes profiles, alerts, evidence, and explanations to users. We define the system as agentic because specialized components have distinct roles and are coordinated through an explicit reasoning process—not because every component is an autonomous LLM. | 6

===== SLIDE 7 =====
EVIDENCE GENERATION | Three evidence agents — deliberately non-equivalent roles | LM Lexicon Agent | Financially interpretable word evidence | • Positive / negative | • Uncertainty | • Litigiousness | • Constraining / modal | Strength: transparent, auditable | Limitation: weak semantic context | FinBERT Agent | Finance-domain contextual sentiment | • P(Negative) | • P(Neutral) | • P(Positive) | • Predicted class | • Model confidence | Strength: semantic context | Limitation: lower direct traceability | Context & Quality Agent | Qualifies informational significance | • Persistence / novelty | • Peer similarity / distinctiveness | • Specificity | • Boilerplate | • Hedging / negation / caution | Strength: disclosure context | Outputs remain separate until adjudication. | 7
-- NOTES: The agents are intentionally not interchangeable. LM contributes transparent lexical evidence. FinBERT contributes contextual sentiment probabilities. The Context and Quality Agent does not independently determine sentiment; it qualifies the informational significance of the text. That distinction is important because ADAM is not a three-vote ensemble. The architecture preserves each evidence type so the adjudicator can reason about both convergence and conflict. | 7

===== SLIDE 8 =====
CONTEXT | What the Context & Quality Agent adds | Year-over-year similarity | Is the language substantially carried over from t−1? | Multi-year persistence | Does similar language persist across t−1 and t−2? | Temporal novelty | Is comparable language absent from recent disclosure history? | Peer commonality / distinctiveness | Is the language common within the sector or unusually firm-specific? | Specificity | Does the passage contain concrete, firm-specific details? | Boilerplate / linguistic qualifiers | Is the passage repetitive, generic, hedged, conditional, negated, or legally cautious? | Key distinction: sentiment answers “what is the tone?”; context answers “how informative is this language here and now?” | 8
-- NOTES: This is the layer that moves the artifact beyond sentiment alone. A negative paragraph may be routine and low-information if it has appeared unchanged for years. A moderately negative paragraph may be much more consequential if it is new, specific, and unique to the firm. The Context and Quality Agent therefore qualifies the evidence rather than flipping sentiment mechanically. In the final paper, the exact model, prompt, retrieval logic, and scoring scales will be documented for reproducibility. | 8

===== SLIDE 9 =====
REASONING | Adjudication separates tone, economics, informativeness, and uncertainty | Linguistic sentiment | Positive • Negative • Neutral | Economic direction | Favorable • Adverse • Mixed • No clear direction | Informativeness | High • Medium • Low | Status | Confidence | Ambiguity | Review needed | Adjudicator asks: Do the signals converge? If not, what explains the conflict — and is the remaining evidence sufficient for a coherent interpretation? | Six primary discrepancy categories | No meaningful conflict | Lexical–semantic mismatch | Boilerplate / generic language | Factual event vs neutral wording | Conditional / negated / mixed language | Unresolved evidence | Market outcomes are not observed by the adjudicator; they are reserved for external validation. | 9
-- NOTES: The adjudicator’s main contribution is construct separation. A sentence can be linguistically neutral yet economically adverse. A passage can be negative but uninformative because it is boilerplate. The adjudicator therefore returns separate fields rather than one sentiment score. It also assigns a primary discrepancy category and can retain ambiguity or unresolved evidence. This is the point at which ADAM becomes a decision-support system rather than simply another classifier. | 9

EVALUATION | Independent human reference set | 300 | paragraphs | 144 Item 1A | 156 Item 7 | 2016–2025 | all 11 sectors | 3 independent reviewers | Linguistic sentiment | Positive • Negative • Neutral | Economic direction | Favorable • Adverse • Mixed • No clear direction | Disclosure informativeness | High • Medium • Low | Interpretive ambiguity | Clear • Ambiguous | Reviewers were blinded to LM, FinBERT, and ADAM outputs. | 11
-- NOTES: The primary evaluation uses 300 paragraphs, each independently reviewed by three human reviewers. Review packets included the target paragraph, surrounding text when available, and relevant prior-year context, but excluded all model outputs. The annotations intentionally separate four constructs. This gives us more than a sentiment label: it lets us test whether the system distinguishes tone, economic direction, informativeness, and interpretive difficulty. | 11

===== SLIDE 12 =====
EVALUATION | Human disagreement confirms the task is genuinely difficult | Linguistic sentiment | 21% | 66% | 13% | Economic direction | 25% | 64% | 11% | Informativeness | 32% | 54% | 14% | All 3 agree | 2 of 3 agree | All differ | Interpretive disagreement is not only annotation noise — it becomes an empirical signal of task difficulty. | Descriptive agreement before reconciliation. -- NOTES: The low unanimous agreement is important rather than embarrassing. Only 21 percent of paragraphs received unanimous sentiment labels, 25 percent for economic direction, and 32 percent for informativeness. Most of the remaining cases still had a two-of-three majority, but the pattern shows that these disclosures are genuinely interpretive. We therefore preserve reviewer disagreement and later ask whether model disagreement and ADAM’s review-needed signals correspond to the same difficult cases. | 12

===== SLIDE 14 =====
PRELIMINARY RESULTS | Linguistic sentiment: ADAM shows a large preliminary gain | PRELIMINARY — VALIDATION ONGOING | LM dictionary | 37.3% | FinBERT | 60.7% | LM–FinBERT ensemble | 58.7% | ADAM (current) | 82.7% | Accuracy | Macro-F1 | LM 0.320   |   FinBERT 0.529   |   Ensemble 0.384   |   ADAM 0.745 | Interpretation | The main claim is architectural: preserve and coordinate evidence rather than average it away. | Final paper will report confirmatory testing, confidence intervals, and paired significance tests. | 14
-- NOTES: These are preliminary validation results and should be presented that way. On the current 300-paragraph reference set, the current ADAM judge reaches 82.7 percent sentiment accuracy and a macro-F1 of 0.745, compared with 60.7 percent accuracy for FinBERT and 37.3 percent for the LM dictionary. The important interpretation is not that one LLM is better than another; it is that structured evidence coordination appears to outperform the conventional baselines. Final confirmatory tests and paired statistical comparisons are still needed. | 14

===== SLIDE 17 =====
ARCHITECTURE TEST | Does the architecture matter — or is the LLM doing all the work? | Hold the adjudicator constant. | 17 | Judge only | Paragraph only | Sentiment   82.0% / .731 | Direction   84.0% / .683 | Info.       67.2% / .453 | Base evidence | Paragraph + LM + FinBERT | Sentiment   82.0% / .730 | Direction   83.7% / .694 | Info.       68.3% / .469 | Full ADAM | + Context & Quality evidence | Sentiment   82.7% / .745 | Direction   83.3% / .689 | Info.       71.4% / .607 | Context adds little for sentiment or economic direction, but materially improves disclosure informativeness. | Informativeness ΔF1: +.138 vs. no-context; +.154 vs. judge-only — both statistically significant.
-- NOTES: This ablation is essential for the design-science claim. We use the same adjudicator in all three configurations. Judge-only tells us what the LLM can do by itself. This is the result that puts a useful boundary around the agentic claim. We hold the adjudicator constant and progressively add evidence. For linguistic sentiment and economic direction, Judge-only, Base Evidence, and Full ADAM perform very similarly. That tells us the LLM itself explains most of the gain on those tasks. Informativeness is different: macro-F1 rises from 0.453 for Judge-only to 0.469 without the Context Agent and to 0.607 for Full ADAM. The increase over both reduced configurations is statistically significant. So the architecture matters most when the construct depends on information outside the focal paragraph—prior filings, peer language, persistence, novelty, and specificity. That task-dependent result is more credible than claiming that adding agents always improves performance. | 17

===== SLIDE 18 =====
DECISION SUPPORT | What does ADAM add beyond a sentiment label? | Prioritize attention | High-informativeness flag | 81.0% precision | 69.9% recall | Trace the reasoning | Complete evidence record for all 300 cases | Paragraph → agents → context → rationale | Route uncertainty | Confidence < .80 | 56% sent to review | 92.3% of errors captured | 97.0% accuracy on automated remainder | Avoid the proxy trap | FinBERT-negative rule | 19.5% precision for High informativeness | vs. ADAM: 81.0% | The DSS value is selective attention + traceability + review routing — not simply a higher sentiment score. | 18
-- NOTES: This slide summarizes the DSS payoff. First, ADAM's High-informativeness flag captures 69.9 percent of the expert-rated High passages with 81 percent precision, while a simple FinBERT-negative rule has only 19.5 percent precision. Second, every validation decision is traceable to the paragraph, agent-level evidence, context indicators, discrepancy diagnosis, and adjudication rationale. Third, confidence can be used to route work selectively: at a 0.80 threshold, 56 percent of paragraphs go to review, 92.3 percent of ADAM's sentiment errors are captured in that review set, and accuracy on the remaining automated decisions rises to 97 percent. The point is not that 0.80 is universally optimal; it illustrates the workload-accuracy trade-off a DSS can expose. We should avoid claiming improved analyst productivity until a user study is conducted. | 18
